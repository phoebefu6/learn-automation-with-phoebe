Leave it running

A workflow with a key, a dead-letter list and an alert survives a bad day. Whether it survives a year depends on things no node setting holds: whose it is, what to do when it breaks at 7am, where it is written down, and when someone last looked at it. Tonight every automation gets an owner, a one-page runbook, a row in one inventory and a review date. Then we look at the state that resets without telling you when an automation is paused or rebuilt, and at the step everybody skips: switching it off when the task it served has gone.

Part 0

Where we start

Session 1 asked how long the task would live; session 5 made the workflow fail safely. Between those two sits the long middle of an automation's life, where nothing is wrong for months and then something small is: a password rotated, a field renamed, a Zap paused for a migration and switched back on. Automations rarely fail loudly in that middle. They go quiet, and the cost of quiet is paid by whoever expected the text, the record or the file.

Part 1 · covers ownership and the runbook

One name, one page

5 min live

Every automation needs a named person who owns it: not a team, not "ops", a person who gets the alert and can switch it off. And every automation needs one page that lets someone else act when that person is asleep. The page is short because the reader is in a hurry, and the most important line on it is the one that says how to replay a failure without sending anything twice.

LiveWhy a person, not a team2 min▶

An alert sent to a team channel is read by everyone and acted on by nobody, a pattern most people who have shared an on-call channel will recognise. Name one person and one backup. The owner does not have to fix every failure; they have to decide, within a working day, whether it is fixed, parked or switched off. The automation's row in the inventory carries their name, and an automation without a name is the first thing the review script flags.

Ownership also answers the question session 1 left open: who knows how long the task will live? The owner is the person who hears first when the report is cancelled or the client leaves.

Self-studyWhat goes on the page, and where the craft lives3 min▶

Seven sections are enough for a no-code workflow: what it does in one line; owner and backup; the trigger and every side effect; how you know it is broken (the alert, an empty or non-empty dead-letter list, a daily count that stops moving); how to pause it, and what happens to events while it is paused; how to replay safely; and where the dead-letter list is and when it is redriven.

"How to replay safely" deserves its own line because the executions list misleads. A lost reply ends red after the message has gone, and the bench counted 11 runs like that on one day. The rule for the page: look the event id up in the receiver's log first, and replay only with the same idempotency key. Writing runbooks that a tired person can follow is a craft of its own, taught in depth in the reliability course's runbooks session.

Real world

The automation the operator forgot how to do by hand. Google's SRE book describes the downside of highly effective automation: "human operators are progressively more relieved of useful direct contact with the system as the automation covers more and more daily activities over time." The small-team version is the invoice workflow that ran for two years: when it stopped, nobody remembered which folder the files used to go into. Keep the manual procedure as section 5's last line, and run it by hand once a year.

Part 2 · covers the inventory

Every automation, one list

5 min live

Three automations fit in your head. Ten do not, and the ones you forget are the ones that fail quietly. One list, one row per automation, with the columns that answer the questions this whole series asked: what triggers it, what it changes in the world, whether a retry can repeat that change, where failures go, who hears about them, and when someone last looked.

LiveThe twelve columns, and the five that get checked3 min▶

The inventory has twelve columns: name, tool, owner, trigger, side effect, idempotency, dead letter, alert to, runbook, last review, review every (days), units a month. The review script checks five things from them: is there an owner, is there a runbook, does a failure reach a person, can a retry repeat the side effect (a side effect with no idempotency), and is the review overdue. Each check maps to one session: the owner and runbook to tonight, the alert and dead letter to session 5, idempotency to sessions 4 and 5, units to session 3, and the review date back to session 1's question about lifetime.

Keep it in one place everybody can read: a sheet, a CSV in a repository, a database table. Two lists will disagree within a quarter, and the automation that is missing from the one people check is the one that will fail unnoticed.

Self-studyUnits a month: the column that catches a bill2 min▶

The bench's dead-letter rung ran 613 executions on one day, about 18,400 a month, which is the number on the Order SMS row. On per-execution billing that sits above n8n's Starter plan as read on 5 October 2026; on Zapier, the same workflow's 714 tasks a day would be about 21,000 a month. Recording units next to the automation means the review notices a tier change before the invoice does. Re-read the pricing page at each review: prices and units change (Make moved from operations to credits on 27 August).

Part 3 · covers state that resets, review cadence, retirement

The year ahead, and the end

5 min live

Some of what makes an automation safe is state that lives somewhere you did not choose: a list of ids a trigger has seen, a store of keys a provider remembers, a dedupe history with a size limit. That state resets on pause, expires on a clock, or rolls over when it fills. A review date is when you check it; a retirement date is when you stop paying for it.

LiveThree pieces of state that reset under you3 min▶

A polling trigger's seen-list. Zapier's platform documentation says that when a polling Zap is first turned on, Zapier makes an initial call to the app and stores each item's id, and when the Zap is turned off, "that list is cleared." Read together, switching a polling Zap off and on again re-learns everything currently in the app as already seen, so items created while it was off do not trigger. Test it on your own Zap before you rely on it, and after any pause, replay the gap deliberately.

A provider's key window. Stripe says idempotency keys can be removed once they are at least 24 hours old, after which a repeat with the same key is treated as a new request. A dead-letter list redriven the same night is protected by its keys; one redriven five days later may not be. Check the receiver's log for each event before a late redrive.

A dedupe history with a size. n8n's Remove Duplicates node keeps 10,000 items by default when it remembers values across executions. A busy workflow rolls past that and forgets the oldest ids; a very late duplicate then gets through.

Self-studyA review that takes fifteen minutes, and when to retire3 min▶

Every 90 days for anything that touches customers, every 180 for internal chores: read the last month of failures and the dead-letter list; check the connection and credentials are still valid; re-read the pricing page and update units a month; confirm the owner is still the owner; ask the owner whether the task still exists. Write the date in the inventory. The PKM course has the habit side of a review that actually happens.

Retire an automation when its task has died, when it fails the break-even line on the numbers you now have, or when nobody will own it. Retiring is four steps: switch it off; export and archive its JSON with the date; revoke the credentials and webhook URLs it held; mark the row retired rather than deleting it, so the next person can see it existed.

Real world

The deprecation notice in the startup log. When the current n8n release starts outside a container, its own log now says that running n8n outside a container is deprecated and that future versions will require the official Docker image. A self-hosted automation that is never reviewed meets that sentence on the day an update stops starting. A review that reads the startup log once a quarter meets it a year early.

Build-along

Inventory, runbook, review date

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Five steps. You will add your own automation to a five-row inventory, run the review script against it, write the runbook page for it, and set the first review date. The script needs only Python 3; the inventory rows besides yours are constructed. Every output shown is what the script prints on the file as shipped.

Write the runbook page. Seven headings, one or two lines each. Section 6 is the one to get exactly right.

★ Step 4 · runbooks/leads.md1. What it does: creates one CRM contact per web-form submission. 2. Owner and backup: <you>, then <someone>. 3. Trigger and side effect: webhook from the form tool; creates a contact. 4. How you know it is broken: alert in <channel>; dead-letter list not empty; daily contact count flat. 5. How to pause it: deactivate the workflow; the form tool retries its webhook (check how long). Manual fallback: export the form responses and import them by hand. 6. Set the review date and walk the final checklist. Put the next review in your calendar with the inventory link in it. Then check your automation against the six sessions, one line each.

★ Step 5 · the final checklist[ ] 1 It passes the break-even line, or the non-time reason is written down. [ ] 2 Every field the action needs survives the trip; reach-backs name nodes that exist. [ ] 3 Units a month are recorded against today's pricing page, with the date. [ ] 4 Reads retry, writes retry only with a key the receiver stores. [ ] 5 Failures park on a list, the list is redriven, and an alert names the event. [ ] 6 It has an owner, a runbook, an inventory row and a review date.

The inventory is the one place. If your team already keeps a list of systems, add the twelve columns to it rather than starting a second list. Two lists that describe the same automations will disagree, and the one nobody checks will be the one that was right.

Homework

After the last session

30 min
  1. Inventory every automation you own or depend on, including the ones someone else built and the browser extensions that act on your behalf. Run the review script on the result.
  2. Fix the top flag the script prints, then re-run it and confirm that row is clean.
  3. Pause and resume test. On a test copy of one polling automation, switch it off, create an item, switch it on, and record whether the item triggered. Write the answer in its runbook.
  4. Retire one automation whose task has died: switch off, archive the JSON with the date, revoke its credentials, mark the row retired.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 6 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · A polling Zap was switched off for a weekend migration and switched back on on Monday. Forms submitted over the weekend did not create records. Why, according to Zapier's documentation?

The seen-list is state outside your workflow. After any pause, replay the gap deliberately and write that step into the runbook. Test the behaviour on your own Zap before relying on it.

2 · A dead-letter list is redriven to Stripe five days after the failures, with the original idempotency keys. What should happen first?

A key protects a repeat only while the receiver remembers it. Late redrives are where key windows matter; the runbook's replay section says to look before resending.

3 · The review script flags "Invoice filing: no owner, no runbook, fails silently, review overdue" and "Order SMS: ok". The Order SMS row has far more runs. Which needs a person first?

An automation that fails loudly gets fixed. One that fails silently fails for as long as nobody looks. Owner and alert first, then runbook, then the review date.
