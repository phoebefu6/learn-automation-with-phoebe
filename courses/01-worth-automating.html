Learn Automation with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Is it worth the time?

Every automation starts as a feeling: "I do this every week and it is boring." Tonight the feeling becomes a line on a chart. On one side, how often the task happens, how many minutes each run takes, and how long the task will still exist. On the other, the hours to build it and the minutes a month to keep it working. Where the two lines cross is the break-even, and most people never draw it. We read xkcd 1205 cell by cell, add the two costs it leaves out, hear the Google SRE argument that time is not the only return, and leave with one task worth building in session 2.

Part 0

Where we start

These six sessions are about automations that do not break: a form that becomes a CRM record, an order that becomes a text message, a file that gets renamed and filed. Before any tool is opened, one question decides whether the rest is worth doing. Is this task frequent enough, long enough, and alive long enough to pay back the hours it takes to build and keep running? The question has arithmetic, and the arithmetic has two holes that cost more than the build.

Part 1 · covers the break-even line, xkcd 1205

Frequency, minutes, lifetime

6 min live

The saving grows a little every time the task happens. The cost arrives mostly at once, as build hours, and then trickles in as upkeep. Draw both against weeks and you get two lines. The saving line starts at zero and climbs; the cost line starts high and climbs slowly. They cross once, if at all, and that week is the only number the decision needs.

LiveThe three numbers on the saving side, and the one people skip3 min▶

How often. Count it, do not remember it. "Every day" is usually four days a week once holidays, quiet weeks and the days someone else did it are taken out. A week of tally marks beats any estimate.

How many minutes. The minutes the automation removes, not the minutes the task takes. If the automation still leaves you to check the result, that check stays on your side of the ledger.

How long the task lives. The number nobody writes down. Tools change, the report gets cancelled, the client leaves, the form gets a new field. A saving of 20 minutes a week is worth 17 hours a year and nothing at all after the task disappears. Ask the person who owns the process how long they expect it to exist, and use the shorter of their answer and three years.

Multiply the three and you have the most you can afford to spend. Compare it with the build hours plus upkeep and you have the decision.

Self-studyReading xkcd 1205 without misreading it4 min▶

Randall Munroe's "Is It Worth the Time?" (xkcd 1205, 29 April 2013) is the same calculation done once for a grid of cases. Its question, paraphrased from the comic: how long can you work on making a routine task more efficient before you are spending more time than you save, across five years. Columns are how often you do the task (50 a day down to yearly); rows are how much time you shave off (1 second up to 1 day). Each cell is the build budget.

Two things are easy to miss. The "days" are 24-hour days, so the 6 days for a daily five-minute task is 152 hours, which is 19 working days of 8 hours. And the chart has no upkeep column: it assumes the improvement, once made, costs nothing to keep. For a keyboard shortcut that is nearly true. For anything that talks to another company's API it is not, and Part 2 is about that gap. The comic is CC BY-NC 2.5; read it at the link rather than here.

Real world

The weekly report that outlived nobody. A common story in operations teams: someone spends two days automating a Friday spreadsheet export, the export runs perfectly for six weeks, and then the team that read it reorganises and stops asking. At one hour a week, six weeks paid back six hours of a sixteen-hour build. Nothing broke. The task simply died before the line crossed. Asking "how long will anyone want this?" first would have pointed at a ten-minute checklist instead.

Part 2 · covers upkeep, the cost of a wrong run, the SRE counterpoint

What the chart leaves out

5 min live

A time-saved chart has one column for cost: building it. A running automation has at least four more. It needs fixing in its first weeks, it needs upkeep when the apps on either end change, it needs someone to look at it now and then, and every so often it does the wrong thing at machine speed. The last one is the cost that turns a good investment into an apology.

LiveFour costs to add before you decide3 min▶

First-week fixes. The first real runs find the cases the test did not: an empty field, a name with an apostrophe, a file uploaded twice. Budget about half the build again.

Upkeep. An automation sits between two products that both ship updates. A renamed field, an expired connection, a changed rate limit: each one is a small repair. Fifteen minutes a month is a reasonable floor for anything that touches two other companies' software.

Broken runs. Steps fail: a timeout, a 500 error, an outage. Someone has to notice, find the run, and fix or replay it. Session 5 is about making that cheap.

Wrong runs. The run that "worked" and did the wrong thing: texted a customer twice, filed an invoice under the wrong client, lost an order without saying so. No timer measures it and it is often the largest cost, because a human doing the task would have noticed. Session 4 counts exactly these on one seeded day.

Self-studyThe counter-argument: time is not the only return3 min▶

Google's SRE book disagrees with any time-only chart, xkcd's included. Chapter 7, "The Evolution of Automation at Google" (Niall Murphy with John Looney and Michael Kacirek), lists four values: consistency, a platform you can extend and fix once, faster repairs, and time saving. Its argument is that automation "provides more than just time saving, so it's worth implementing in more cases than a simple time-expended versus time-saved calculation might suggest." A task done by hand a hundred times is not done the same way a hundred times; a script is.

The same chapter also says "doing automation thoughtlessly can create as many problems as it solves," and opens with "For SRE, automation is a force multiplier, not a panacea." Both sides are right about different things. The break-even line is the honest floor: if a task fails it on time, the automation has to be justified by something else, and that something should be said out loud ("we automate this because the manual version gets the VAT wrong one time in twenty"), not smuggled in. The engineering view of repeated manual work as toil, and how teams budget for removing it, belongs to the reliability course's toil session.

Real world

xkcd answered itself nine months later. In xkcd 1319 "Automation" (January 2014) the same author drew the plan, where writing the code takes a while and then frees up time, against what actually happens, where debugging and rethinking take over and the original task never gets done. It is a joke and also a fair description of the first-week-fixes line on the right-hand receipt above. Put both comics on the wall and the decision is honest.

Part 3 · covers choosing the first candidate

Which task goes first

4 min live

The break-even line ranks tasks by time. A second question ranks them by how safely a machine can do them: are the steps the same every time? A task that is frequent and rule-like is the first candidate. A task that is frequent and needs judgement wants help, not a robot. A task that is rare and rule-like wants a checklist. Some tasks want nothing at all.

LiveThe one-sentence test2 min▶

Write the candidate as "When X happens, do Y with Z." When a web form is submitted, create a contact in the CRM with the name, email and source. When an invoice PDF lands in the inbox folder, rename it to date-client-amount and move it to the client's folder. If the sentence needs an "unless", a "depending on" or "if it looks like", the task has judgement in it, and that part either stays with a person or becomes an explicit rule before anything is built.

The X is the trigger, the Y is the action, and the Z is the data that has to travel between them. Session 2 is those three words, in detail.

Self-studyWhere judgement goes instead2 min▶

Tasks in the bottom-right corner, frequent but judgement-heavy, are where language models are most often put to work: drafting the reply, sorting the inbox, summarising the thread, with a person approving before anything is sent. That is a different discipline with its own failure modes, and it has its own course: AI-Powered Productivity. When the steps themselves are chosen by a model at run time, the result is an agent rather than a workflow; the agents course draws that line. Everything in these six sessions is a workflow: the steps are fixed when you build it.

Build-along

Price five of your own tasks

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Five steps. The first two are on paper or in a notes app; the next two run a twelve-line Python script you can paste into any Python 3 (or skip, and use the calculator above, which does the same arithmetic). The last step writes the sentence session 2 will turn into a workflow. The printed outputs below are what the script prints on the example tasks; yours will differ, and should.

List what you repeat. Five tasks you did more than once last week, at work or at home. Do not filter yet. If you kept no tally, scroll your sent mail and your calendar for the last seven days: repetition shows up there.

★ Step 1 · the raw list1. copy web-form leads into the CRM 2. send the Monday status email 3. rename and file invoice PDFs 4. post the weekly sales number in the team chat 5. tidy the yearly tax folder

Put four numbers on each. Times a week, minutes the automation would remove each time, hours you think it would take to build, minutes of upkeep a month. Guess the last two; you will refine them after session 3.

★ Step 2 · numbers, not feelingstask per week minutes build h upkeep min/month leads into the CRM 25 3 4 20 Monday status email 1 15 2 10 invoice PDFs 10 2 3 15 weekly sales number 1 10 3 30 yearly tax folder 1/52 120 6 0

Run the line for all five. Paste this into a file called worth.py with your own rows and run python3 worth.py. Three years is the default lifetime; change YEARS if the owner of the process gave you a shorter answer.

★ Step 3 · worth.pyTASKS = [ # name, times a week, minutes each, build hours, upkeep minutes a month ("Copy web-form leads into the CRM", 25, 3, 4, 20), ("Send the Monday status email", 1, 15, 2, 10), ("Rename and file invoice PDFs", 10, 2, 3, 15), ("Post the weekly sales number", 1, 10, 3, 30), ("Tidy the yearly tax folder", 1 / 52, 120, 6, 0), ] YEARS = 3 for name, per_week, mins, build_h, upkeep_m in TASKS: saved = per_week * mins * 52 * YEARS / 60 spent = build_h + upkeep_m * 12 * YEARS / 60 weekly = per_week * mins / 60 - upkeep_m * 12 / 52 / 60 be = "never" if weekly <= 0 else f"{build_h / weekly:.0f} weeks" print(f"{name:32s} saved {saved:6.1f} h spent {spent:5.1f} h net {saved - spent:+6.1f} h break-even {be}") # Copy web-form leads into the CRM saved 195.0 h spent 16.0 h net +179.0 h break-even 3 weeks # Send the Monday status email saved 39.0 h spent 8.0 h net +31.0 h break-even 9 weeks # Rename and file invoice PDFs saved 52.0 h spent 12.0 h net +40.0 h break-even 11 weeks # Post the weekly sales number saved 26.0 h spent 21.0 h net +5.0 h break-even 59 weeks # Tidy the yearly tax folder saved 6.0 h spent 6.0 h net +0.0 h break-even 156 weeks

Check your arithmetic against xkcd. Three cells of the comic, recomputed. If your function gives 6.3 for a daily five-minute task, it agrees with the chart's 6 days, and you now know those are 24-hour days.

★ Step 4 · xkcd.pydef budget_days(times_per_day, seconds_saved, years=5): """How long you can spend, in 24-hour days, before the work costs more than it saves.""" return times_per_day * seconds_saved * 365 * years / 86400 print(round(budget_days(1, 300), 1)) # 5 minutes, daily -> chart says 6 days print(round(budget_days(1 / 7, 3600), 1)) # 1 hour, weekly -> chart says 10 days print(round(budget_days(5, 30), 1)) # 30 seconds, 5 a day -> chart says 3 days print(round(budget_days(1, 300) * 3, 1)) # the same 5 minutes in 8-hour working days # 6.3 # 10.9 # 3.2 # 19.0

Circle one and write its sentence. Take the highest net hours whose sentence has no "unless". On the example list that is the CRM leads: 179 hours net, break-even in 3 weeks, and the same steps every time. The weekly sales number nets only 5 hours over three years and breaks even in week 59; it is a candidate only if consistency matters more than time, and you should say so.

★ Step 5 · the sentenceWhen a web form is submitted, create a contact in the CRM with name, email, company and the form's source field. Lifetime guess: 3 years (asked the sales lead). Wrong-run risk: a duplicate contact if the form is submitted twice.

Write the wrong-run risk next to the sentence. "A duplicate contact if the form is submitted twice" is a one-line note today and the whole subject of session 5. Naming it before you build is the cheapest version of the fix.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Keep a tally for five working days of the one task you circled. Replace your guessed "times a week" with the count and re-run step 3. Most guesses are high.
  2. Ask the owner how long the task will exist. One question, one answer, written next to the sentence. If nobody owns it, write that down too; session 6 is about why that matters.
  3. Find the trigger in the real tool. Open the app where X happens (the form tool, the inbox, the shared folder) and find out whether it can call a webhook, or only be checked on a schedule. Session 2 starts from that answer.
  4. Find one task that fails the line but that you would still automate, and write the non-time reason in one sentence.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · xkcd 1205 says a task done daily that you can shave 5 minutes off is worth up to 6 days of work. What are those 6 days?

5 minutes x 365 x 5 years = 9,125 minutes = 152 hours = 6.3 days of 24 hours. In 8-hour days it is 19. The chart is a five-year build budget and assumes the improvement costs nothing to keep.

2 · Which cost does a time-saved chart leave out that is usually paid by someone other than the builder?

Upkeep and broken runs cost the builder hours. A wrong run costs a customer, a colleague or the books, and no timer catches it. Sessions 4 and 5 measure and then shrink exactly that line.

3 · The weekly sales number nets 5 hours over three years and breaks even in week 59. A colleague says automate it anyway, because the manual number is sometimes wrong. Who is right?

The two sources disagree on purpose. xkcd 1205 counts time only; Google's SRE chapter says automation "provides more than just time saving". Neither cancels the other. Decide on both, and write down which one carried the decision.
